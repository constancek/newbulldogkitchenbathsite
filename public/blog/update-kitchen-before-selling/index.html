Should you update the kitchen before selling? Where the money goes in a house you'll leave in two or three years

By James Smith, Lead Technician · September 25, 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • In a house you'll sell in two or three years, the kitchen money goes to repairs a buyer's inspector would write up, then to the surfaces a buyer sees first.
  • Cabinet fronts, counters, the backsplash, the sink and the faucet change how the room looks without tearing it out.
  • In a national 2025 study of resale value, a minor kitchen remodel that kept the cabinet boxes got back a far larger share of its cost than a major remodel with new cabinets.
  • New cabinet boxes, layout changes and a bigger island are money you spend for the next owner.

The most expensive mistake in a kitchen you're about to leave is new cabinets. Most advice about whether to update the kitchen before selling says the kitchen sells the house, so put the money there. That's half right. A buyer does look hard at the kitchen. What they look at is the doors, the counters, the wall behind the range and the sink, and none of that needs the cabinet boxes to come off the wall.

Say you're a couple in a 1994 two-story in West Chester. The kids are gone, and in two or three years you'll move to something smaller in Mason or over in Florence, Kentucky. The kitchen has the oak cabinets it came with, a laminate counter with a seam by the sink, and a faucet that drips if you don't push the handle all the way down. You'll get three bids, and you want to know which of them is worth the money. Here's where we'd put it, and where we wouldn't.

The usual advice: redo the kitchen, it sells the house

The common view is that a dated kitchen costs you at the sale, so you should remodel it before you list. There's something to that. Realtors told the National Association of Realtors that a kitchen upgrade was one of the projects where they'd seen the biggest rise in buyer demand, cited by 48 percent of them in the 2025 Remodeling Impact Report. Homeowners in the same report gave a kitchen upgrade a Joy Score of 10, the top of its 1 to 10 scale.

So buyers want a good kitchen, and owners love a new one. The leap people make from there is that a full tear-out is the way to get it. The numbers don't back that leap.

What the national numbers say about kitchens and resale

The best-known study of this is the Cost vs. Value Report, published by the Journal of Light Construction. Its resale figures come from a survey of more than 6,000 Realtors, who were shown each project and asked what it adds to the sale price of a home. These are national averages across 119 markets, not Cincinnati prices and not Bulldog's.

The report's "minor kitchen remodel, midrange" keeps the cabinet boxes in place and puts new shaker-style fronts and drawer fronts on them, with new hardware. It also replaces the laminate counters, adds a midpriced sink and faucet, new appliances, a new resilient floor and paint, in a 200-square-foot kitchen with 30 feet of cabinets. In the 2025 national figures, that project recouped 112.9 percent of its cost at resale.

The "major kitchen remodel, midrange" in the same kitchen puts in new semi-custom cabinets and a 3-by-5-foot island. It recouped 50.9 percent nationally. The upscale version of the major remodel recouped 35.7 percent. The major remodel cost the study's sample job roughly three times what the minor one did.

Read that as a pattern, not a promise. An estimate of resale value isn't a sale price, and your house sells on the comparable sales on your street. The pattern is clear enough, though. The study's job that kept the cabinet boxes, even with new appliances, flooring and paint in it, got back a larger share of its cost, by a wide margin, than the job that replaced the cabinets.

Paint and the roof come before the kitchen

Here's the part that may delay a kitchen job, and we'd still tell you. When the same NAR report asked Realtors what sellers should do before listing, the top answers were painting the whole house (50 percent), painting one room (41 percent) and a new roof (37 percent), according to the 2025 Remodeling Impact Report. Kitchen work wasn't in those top three.

If the roof on your 1994 two-story is original, a buyer's inspector is likely to write it up before they open a kitchen drawer. Fix that first. Then come back to the kitchen.

How to update the kitchen before selling: fix, then freshen

Kitchen money in a house you're leaving goes out in two rounds. The first is anything a buyer's inspector would put in the report. The second is the handful of surfaces a buyer looks at in the first minute.

Leaks, soft sink base, dead outletsYes, firstThese show up in the inspection report and in the negotiation

Round one: what a buyer's inspector writes up

Start under the sink with a flashlight. Look for a drip at the trap or the supply lines, a water stain on the cabinet floor, and wood that's swollen or soft when you press it. A soft sink base is the one cabinet problem that changes the whole plan. Can my cabinets be refaced? walks through a five-minute check of the sink base, the shelves and the face frames.

Then go around the counter and try every outlet. A dead outlet, or one near the sink that doesn't trip when you press its test button, is a call to an electrician, and it costs less to fix now than to argue about after the inspection. Run the dishwasher and the disposal, and look for water on the floor afterward.

None of this is a remodel. It's a short list, and it's the money that keeps a buyer's repair request short.

Round two: what a buyer sees first

Stand in the doorway the way a buyer will at the open house. In a 1994 kitchen, what you see is the cabinet doors, the counter, the strip of wall between them and the sink. Those four things decide whether the kitchen reads as dated or as fine, and each can change without the others.

Cabinet doors: reface them, don't replace the boxes

If the boxes are solid, refacing puts new doors, drawer fronts and veneer on the cabinets you have. The layout stays the same and so does the plumbing. We reface with shaker, flat-panel or raised-panel doors in painted, stained or two-tone finishes. For honey oak from the 90s, reface, refinish or paint compares the three ways to change the color and what each does to oak's open grain. Painting is the one you can do yourself, and in a house you're leaving, that's a fair choice too.

Counters: new tops on the old cabinets

A laminate counter with a burn ring and a lifting seam is one of the first things a buyer notices. Quartz, granite or solid surface can go on older base cabinets if they're flat and sound. New countertops on old cabinets covers what the boxes have to carry. We install quartz, granite and solid-surface tops and digitally template your existing layout before the new top is made.

Backsplash: the wall between counter and cabinet

If your counters are staying, a new backsplash can go up on its own. What decides that job is the back edge of the counter and the outlets, and a backsplash without new countertops goes through both. We can put in a backsplash on its own and coordinate it with the counters and cabinets you're keeping.

Sink and faucet

A new faucet is the smallest job here, and a buyer turns it on. If the sink is chipped or stained, it can come out with the old counter, and a new sink goes in with the new one. We install undermount, farmhouse, drop-in and double-bowl sinks.

Where the money doesn't go

Some kitchen spending makes sense if you're staying 20 years and little sense if you're leaving in three.

  • New cabinet boxes in sound condition. In the study above, the job with new cabinets cost the most and got back the smallest share.
  • Moving the sink or the range. Plumbing and gas moves add cost a buyer won't see.
  • A bigger island or a knocked-out wall. That's a layout for the next owner's taste, and they may want a different one.
  • Strong colors. A navy island or a bold backsplash might be what you'd pick for yourself. In a house you're selling, the buyer has to picture their own things in it.

The exception is a cabinet that's failed: a sink base that rotted out, or doors hanging off broken face frames. Then you're back in round one, and the fix may be replacement.

When to do it: now, not the month before you list

If you're selling in two or three years, we'd do the kitchen work now. You get to use the new counters and the faucet that doesn't drip for two years before someone else does. A job squeezed into the month before listing means taking whatever opening a contractor has, and living in the work while you get the house in shape to show.

Plan the time, too. Many kitchen updates take a few days. New cabinets take longer, with a schedule your project coordinator lays out before work starts.
