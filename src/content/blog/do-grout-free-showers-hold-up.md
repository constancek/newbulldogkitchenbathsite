## How long acrylic shower walls last

The manufacturer behind our walls doesn't publish a lifespan in years, so we won't invent one. What it does publish is a lifetime warranty and a list of what the material resists.

Our walls come from Bath Concepts, which has been in business [since 1999](https://bathconcepts.com/about) and carries a Made in the USA badge. Its FAQ describes the products as made from ["high-quality acrylic materials"](https://bathconcepts.com/faqs) and calls them "non-porous, high-tech polymer materials," which is why you'll see both words for the same wall. On our site, and in the rest of this post, it's solid-surface. Its shower page says the polymer ["won't chip, stain, or fade under normal use."](https://bathconcepts.com/products/showers/)

Hold on to "under normal use." Most of what follows comes down to that phrase, and to the handful of things in a bathroom that count as abnormal for a solid-surface wall.

For comparison, the tiled showers our crews tear out are, in most cases, 20 years old or more. The tile face on most of them is fine. The grout and the board behind it are the reason the shower is coming out, which [the signs your shower needs replacing](/blog/signs-shower-needs-replacing/) walks through stage by stage.

## Scratches on solid-surface shower walls

The wall resists scratches, and resisting is a matter of degree. Bath Concepts calls its material ["scratch-, chip-, stain-, and fade-resistant"](https://bathconcepts.com/products/showers/), and the care instructions name what to keep off it: abrasive pads and gritty cleaners.

The culprits are the ones under your kitchen sink: scouring powder, the green side of a sponge, steel wool, and cream cleansers with "scrub" on the label. Grit takes the gloss off a solid-surface wall a little at a time. A year of hard Saturdays with a gritty cleaner can leave a dull patch at shoulder height where you leaned into it.

A test you can do at the store: rub a dab of the cleaner between your fingers. If it feels sandy, it doesn't go on the shower.

## Stains and soap film on a grout-free wall

Stains have nowhere to go. The surface is non-porous, so water, soap and shampoo sit on top of the wall instead of soaking in, and the manufacturer says the polymer [won't stain under normal use](https://bathconcepts.com/products/showers/).

Tile is a different story at the joints. Cement grout is porous underneath its hard face, and over the years it takes in soap and minerals. [Tile vs. acrylic shower walls](/blog/tile-vs-acrylic-shower-walls/) covers what that means over 20 years.

The white specks you'll still see are minerals from the tap water drying on the surface. They wipe off. [How to clean a grout-free shower](/blog/how-to-clean-grout-free-shower/) has the weekly routine and the trick for the glass door.

## Fading, yellowing and discoloring

The manufacturer says the material won't fade under normal use, and its FAQ calls the products ["highly resistant to cracking, chipping, peeling, and fading under normal use."](https://bathconcepts.com/faqs) None of the pages we read use the word "yellowing" one way or the other, so we won't put words in their mouth.

If the yellowed surface you remember was a cultured-marble vanity top or an old fiberglass tub kit, those are different materials from the solid-surface walls we install. If the color of your shower wall changes in a way you didn't expect, call us and check the written warranty, covered below.

## Cracking and flexing

Bath Concepts lists cracking with chipping and peeling as things the material is [highly resistant to under normal use](https://bathconcepts.com/faqs). The panel that cracks in people's memories is the thin hardware-store kit that gives when you lean on it, trimmed to fit an alcove it wasn't made for. ## Cleaners that can damage acrylic and solid-surface walls

Mild soap, warm water and a soft cloth are what the walls need. Abrasive pads and harsh chemicals are the two things to keep off them, and our crew leaves written care instructions on install day that say so.

- Keep off the walls: scouring powder, scrub pads, steel wool, and cleaners with grit you can feel.
- Keep off the walls: harsh chemicals, including anything you'd use to strip paint or take off nail polish.
- Fine for the walls: a mild liquid soap in a spray bottle, a soft cloth, and a rinse with the handheld.

For anything stronger than soap, check the care sheet first, or call us at (513) 657-3750 and ask before you open the bottle. A spill is worth rinsing off right away so it doesn't sit on the surface.

## Mold and mildew on a shower with no grout

No grout lines means nothing for mildew to grow in. Bath Concepts says its ["non-porous surfaces naturally resist mold and mildew."](https://bathconcepts.com/products/showers/)

The room still steams up, and soap film left on any wall for weeks gives mildew something to hold onto. A weekly wipe-down and a bathroom fan that runs after showers take care of it. If the fan in your hall bath rattles and won't hold a square of toilet paper to the grille, replacing it helps whatever walls you have.

## When tile holds up better

Tile is a good product, and a glazed tile face can take harder scrubbing than a solid-surface wall should get. A tiled shower built with a real waterproofing layer behind it lasts a long time. Its weak spot is the grout, which needs sealing and can still go gray along the bottom row. If you want a hand-set mosaic or a curbless wet room, hire a tile contractor and ask to see the waterproofing before the tile covers it. Bulldog doesn't sell tile, and that's still the right advice for that bathroom.
