You have been through the [colors and patterns page](/inspiration-colors-patterns/) on the iPad, and the Calcutta marble looks fine on a screen. Then you picture it in the master bath of your ranch in Anderson Township, and the picture that comes up instead is the glossy white tub kit in your sister's rental, the one that flexes when you lean on it. "Won't it look like a plastic insert?" is the question we hear most at the kitchen table, in those words or close to them, and it is a fair one. Some acrylic shower walls look cheap. We have pulled a lot of them out of houses around Cincinnati. Five things make them look that way, and each one is something you can check on a made-to-fit surround before you take our word that ours is different.

## The ones that do look like a plastic insert

Two products earned the reputation. The first is the tub wall kit from the home center: three or five thin panels, high-gloss white, with a soap dish and a pair of shelves molded into the back wall. It comes in a stock size, so whoever installs it trims it to meet your tub, and the edge where it stops meets painted drywall with a bead of caulk. Its cousin is the one-piece fiberglass unit a builder set in a 1990s two-story in West Chester, tub and walls molded together, the gel-coat (the glossy outer skin) gone the color of weak tea.

The second is the liner some franchise companies glue over your existing tub and tile. It keeps the old wall in place, and whatever is wet behind it, and puts a layer of shine on top.

Those two are what people mean by "plastic insert." If that were what we installed, you would be right to pass.

## Why acrylic shower walls look cheap: the five tells

Stand in front of one of those and the tells repeat from house to house.

1. **Gloss.** High-gloss white reads as plastic from across the room. The shine shows the ripples in a thin panel and the water spots on it.
2. **Flex.** The panel is thin and there is air or old tile behind it. Press it with your palm and it gives, and you hear it.
3. **Fit.** The kit was made for a tub that is not yours. The panels stop short of the ceiling with a painted strip above them, and the vertical edge ends in caulk against the drywall.
4. **Seams.** The back wall is two or three panels that overlap, so there is a lip across the wall at eye level, and it collects soap.
5. **The extras.** A molded soap dish, a corner shelf stuck on with adhesive, and a framed aluminum sliding door whose bottom track holds water.

The look comes from the panel being thin and made in one size, more than from the material itself.

## What a made-to-fit surround does about each one

The product we install is a solid-surface wall system, fabricated to the measurements of your tub or shower area and installed by the same crew that measured it. Take the five tells in the same order.

The gloss is gone because the finish is a stone pattern. The marble and travertine looks most of our customers pick are a low-sheen panel with veining in it, so from the doorway your eye lands on the veining rather than on a shine. White is there if you want it, in matte or gloss, and so are tile-look patterns in the same material: [subway, herringbone and hexagonal](/inspiration-colors-patterns/), with the lines molded into one non-porous panel and no grout in them.

The flex goes away when the panel is set over a prepared substrate. On install day our crew takes the old tile or the old kit out to the studs, deals with what is behind it, puts up a solid backing, and sets the walls against that. Press on it and it does not move.

The fit is right because the walls, base and trim are built to your room's numbers before the truck arrives. A consultant measures at the consultation. The panels meet in the corners and finish at the edges with trim rather than a bead of caulk against paint.

The seams sit in the corners rather than across the back wall. The back wall is one piece, so there is no lip at eye level and nothing to catch soap mid-wall.

The extras are built in at the shop rather than stuck on afterward: a recessed niche for the shampoo, a built-in seat that reads as a shelf, a shelf or towel bar where you want one. The door is glass, 1/4 to 3/8 inch, frameless if you want it, with hardware in brushed nickel, matte black, oil-rubbed bronze or chrome to match the faucet.

## The doorway test and the six-inch test

Judge it the way you would judge a countertop. Stand in the doorway first. Your eye should go to the veining and the glass, and you should have to hunt for the corner seams. The door hardware should match the faucet. The top edge should finish with trim, square and clean.

Then walk up and stand six inches from the back wall. At six inches you will know it is not quarried marble, and so will we. It is a marble-look panel, and we will not tell you otherwise. The questions at six inches are different ones: does the surface read as one solid piece, is the corner seam tight and the caulk line straight, is the trim square to the wall. Run your hand along it. A thin kit feels like the lid of a cooler; a solid-surface panel has the heft of a countertop under your hand.

Then do the test that counts for more than either. Ask us to show you a finished shower in a ranch like yours, with the original window over the tub and the almond floor tile still down, rather than a showroom display under good lighting. A surround that looks right in a 1968 hall bath in Anderson Township is the one to judge.

## The choices that decide how it reads

Two decisions do most of the work. The first is the pattern. Calcutta marble, white with gray veining, brightens a small bath and goes with the white toilet and vanity top you already have. Travertine, warmer and with less contrast, sits better next to an almond or beige floor you are keeping. If you miss the look of tile, the 3x6 subway pattern gives you the lines without the joints. The consultant goes over the finishes with you at the consultation, and you can see the full list on the [colors and patterns page](/inspiration-colors-patterns/).

The second is the door. A framed aluminum slider on a bottom track is what you remember from the rental, and it is most of why the rental looked the way it did. A frameless glass door with brushed nickel or matte black hardware, and the water-beading coating on the glass, changes the whole wall behind it. If you are keeping the tub, a curtain rod is an option too.

Those two choices, more than the panel itself, decide whether a visitor standing in the doorway sees a shower or an insert.
