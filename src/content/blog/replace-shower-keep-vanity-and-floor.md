The vanity in your hall bath went in around 2015, when you gave up on the cultured-marble top with the crack in it. The floor is the vinyl that came with the house, and it's held up fine. The shower is the problem: gray grout, a pink line on the caulk, and a tub wall you'd like to stop stepping over. So you typed "replace shower only, not whole bathroom," half expecting every contractor to say the room has to go.

In many older bathrooms it doesn't. You can replace the tub or shower area by itself and keep the vanity, the floor and the toilet, as long as three things hold: the floor is solid past the tub, the shower stays where it is, and the plumbing stays on the wall it's on now. Then there's the floor edge, the question most people forget to ask.

## What "only the shower" means on the job

Contractors call the tub or shower area the wet area. Replacing only the wet area means the old tile, the board behind it and the old tub or shower base come out, down to the studs. The new base, walls, valve trim, showerhead and door go in. The vanity, the toilet and the floor in the rest of the room stay where they are.

That scope is what our crews do in one day. They take the wet area to the studs in the morning, see the subfloor and the pipes with the wall open, and have the new base, walls and fixtures in by evening. You take your first shower the next morning, once the caulk has set. An adult needs to be home at the start and end of the day. [What to expect on a one-day bathroom remodel](/blog/one-day-bathroom-remodel-what-to-expect/) walks through that day with the clock on it.

The floor, the vanity and the toilet are separate, longer jobs when they're part of the plan. Know which side of that line you're on before anyone measures.

## When to replace the shower only, not the whole bathroom

Keeping the rest of the room makes sense when all of these are true.

- **The floor is solid past the tub.** Press with your heel along the tub apron (the finished front panel of the tub) and around the base of the toilet. No give anywhere outside the tub's own footprint.
- **You like the vanity, or can live with it.** A vanity from the last ten or fifteen years with a sound top and drawers that close is not a reason to redo the room.
- **The shower stays in the same spot.** Same alcove, same wall for the valve, same general spot for the drain.
- **The toilet sits tight.** It doesn't rock when you sit down, and there's no stain on the floor around it.

A 1968 ranch in Anderson Township with a dry floor and a vanity the kids helped pick out is the plainest version of this. The shower is the part that's failing, so the shower is the part that comes out. [The signs your shower needs replacing](/blog/signs-shower-needs-replacing/) goes through how to tell whether the wall behind the tile is wet, in the order an older shower fails.

## When keeping the vanity and floor doesn't work

Three situations turn a shower job into a bigger one. Better to find them at the kitchen table than on install day.

### A soft floor that runs past the tub

Water that gets past the tub's caulk line and the joint at the apron ends up in the subfloor, the plywood or boards under the vinyl. Some of that damage sits under the tub, where the crew sees it once the tub is out. Ask how wet wood in the tub's footprint is handled in your written quote.

The bigger question is what's past the footprint. If the floor gives a foot out from the apron, or around the toilet, the soft wood runs under the finish floor in the open part of the room. Fixing it means pulling up that floor, and often pulling the toilet to get at the wood around its flange (the ring that holds it to the floor). That's a floor job, and it runs longer than the shower. [Soft floor next to your bathtub](/blog/soft-floor-next-to-bathtub/) has a fill test and a basement check that tell you where the water is coming from and how far it's gone.

### A layout change

Moving the shower to a different wall, widening the alcove into a linen closet, or turning a tub into a longer shower that runs into the room are layout changes. Layout changes take longer than a one-day replacement, and we tell you that up front. The floor and sometimes the vanity get involved, because they sit where the new shower wants to go.

### Plumbing moves

If the shower valve has to move to another wall, or the drain has to move somewhere the floor framing doesn't allow from below, that's a plumbing move. It opens walls and floor outside the wet area, and it also takes longer.

## The floor edge where the old tub meets the floor

In many older bathrooms, the tub went in first and the floor was laid up to it, so the vinyl or tile stops at the apron. If the new base has the same footprint as the old tub, the new edge lands about where the old one did. If the base is shallower, sits at a different depth, or you're trading a tub for a shower base with a low threshold, a strip of floor that was under or behind the old apron may show. That strip might be bare subfloor, an old cut edge, or tile that never got finished because nobody expected to see it.

How that strip gets finished depends on the bathroom and the base you choose, so ask before you sign:

1. Will the new base cover the same footprint as the old tub, or leave floor showing? How wide a strip?
2. What goes on that strip, and is it in my quote?
3. My floor tile at the tub edge is cracked or loose. Does that change the plan?
4. Is any part of my floor coming up, and should it be tested before it does?

The last question matters in an older house. The EPA lists [vinyl floor tiles and the backing on vinyl sheet flooring and adhesives](https://www.epa.gov/asbestos/learn-about-asbestos) among the places asbestos can be found. Its homeowner guidance says to [leave undamaged asbestos-containing materials alone](https://www.epa.gov/asbestos/protect-your-family-exposures-asbestos) and not to sand or level asbestos flooring or its backing. A floor that stays down and undisturbed is one more reason the shower-only plan suits a lot of 1960s houses.

One more thing to do this weekend: check the basement shelves and the attic for a box of spare floor tile. A Montgomery or Fort Thomas, Kentucky house with only one or two owners may still have one from the first job. Almond and pink tile from 1968 is hard to find in a store, and a few spare pieces make any repair at the edge easier for whoever does it.

## Keeping the vanity now and replacing it later

If the vanity is in good shape, keep it. If you think you'll want a new one in a year or two, that's a separate job too. The thing to plan now is color. Pick the shower walls with the vanity top and the floor in the room. ## Doing the shower first and the rest of the room later

Some couples do the shower this year and the floor and vanity the next. That works, with one condition: if the floor is soft, the floor can't wait. A new shower base sitting next to wet subfloor is a new shower with an old problem beside it.
